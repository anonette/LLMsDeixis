# Before Alignment Speaks: Cultural and Linguistic Pre-Alignment in Yoruba Ethical Reasoning by Multilingual LLMs

> **Historical draft (2 models, pre–ẹ̀mí disambiguation).** Current analysis: [`Yoruba_Four_Model_Complete_Analysis_Report.md`](Yoruba_Four_Model_Complete_Analysis_Report.md) · [`DOCUMENTATION_INDEX.md`](DOCUMENTATION_INDEX.md).

## Abstract

This paper develops the concept of cultural and linguistic pre-alignment to describe a form of prior shaping in multilingual large language models: before explicit task alignment, models already carry learned expectations about how moral voice, stance, and ethical legitimacy should sound in a language. Using Yoruba ethical responses generated by GPT-4o and Claude-3.5 Sonnet, I argue that multilingual models do not merely translate English moral discourse into Yoruba. Rather, they enter Yoruba through locally meaningful resources of personhood, address, and moral positioning. The clearest evidence lies in the distinction between regular first-person `mo` and emphatic first-person `emi`, in the strong recurrence of advisory formulas such as `O yẹ kí...`, and in the deployment of culturally resonant moral lexicon such as `ìwà` and `ọkàn`. These patterns suggest that model behavior is not only technically multilingual but already unevenly socialized by the distributions of discourse it has absorbed. I argue that this matters not simply for NLP evaluation but for the politics of AI legitimacy: systems may sound culturally attuned before they are ethically trustworthy. The paper therefore reframes multilingual alignment as a question not only of safety and preference optimization, but also of pre-existing discursive inheritance.

## 1. Introduction: What Speaks Before Alignment?

Alignment discourse often imagines a sequence. First comes pretraining: large-scale ingestion of text. Then comes alignment: instruction tuning, safety layers, preference modeling, constitutional guidance, and other post hoc techniques for shaping behavior. This sequence is analytically useful, but it can hide something important. Before any explicit alignment intervention, models may already have learned not only lexical associations and genre conventions, but also culturally specific expectations about how authority, hesitation, care, and moral self-positioning are performed.

This paper is about that prior layer. I call it **pre-alignment**.

The term does not mean that models are ethically aligned in any substantive or normative sense. It means, more modestly and more materially, that they arrive already conditioned by discourse distributions that make some forms of moral speech more available than others. A multilingual model does not approach all languages as blank channels for the same content. It approaches them as differently structured fields of social possibility.

Yoruba makes this visible with unusual clarity. The distinction between regular first-person `mo` and emphatic first-person `emi` or `èmi` gives the language a morphological resource for differentiating reflective positioning from personal insistence, analytic commentary from owned decision. English lacks such a direct contrast. If a model systematically exploits this difference in ethical reasoning, then what we are seeing is not merely translation. We are seeing the uptake of a culturally and linguistically specific technology of moral subjectivity.

The question, then, is not just whether models can speak Yoruba. It is whether they speak it as if Yoruba already carries expectations about what ethical speech should feel like.

## 2. Pre-Alignment as Discursive Inheritance

The idea of pre-alignment proposed here draws together three traditions that are too rarely placed in sustained contact.

First, linguistic anthropology and pragmatics have long shown that pronouns, deictics, indexicals, and address forms do not merely refer; they position speakers within a moral and social order (Benveniste, 1971; Silverstein, 1976; Duranti, 2009). Saying `I` is never only saying who speaks. It is also a way of claiming, softening, displacing, or intensifying responsibility.

Second, the Sapir-Whorf tradition, in its strongest and weakest forms alike, insists that language does not simply encode thought after the fact. It conditions habitual pathways of expression and attention (Sapir, 1929; Whorf, 1956). Whatever one thinks of classical relativity claims, it is difficult to deny that languages differ in the kinds of stance and relation they make easy, obligatory, or elegant.

Third, contemporary work on foundation models has made clear that large models are sedimented archives of textual regularity rather than transparent reasoning engines (Bender et al., 2021; Bommasani et al., 2021). Their outputs inherit genre, prestige, bias, cliché, and discourse habit. If so, then multilingual models must also inherit differently weighted expectations about what counts as plausible moral discourse in different languages.

Pre-alignment names this inheritance at the level of discourse form.

It is what happens when the model arrives already carrying priors about:

- how forcefully a speaker should enter a moral judgment,
- whether guidance should sound advisory or abstract,
- whether personhood is staged as conviction, relation, procedure, or analysis,
- and what local lexicon is available for making ethical seriousness recognizable.

## 3. Why Yoruba Matters

Yoruba is not useful here because it is exotic, but because it is analytically clarifying. It makes visible a distinction that English largely obscures.

The project results show that the models recurrently differentiate between `mo` and `emi`. The former often appears in reflective or analytic formulations such as `Mo rò pé...` (`I think that...`). The latter appears more often in moments of owned decision or contrast, as in `Èmi yóò pinnu...` (`I [emphatic] will decide...`).

This is not a trivial surface feature. It is a resource for calibrating moral presence. It lets the speaker mark how fully the self is entering the judgment.

In the Yoruba corpus analyzed here, Claude-3.5 shows a mean emphatic ratio of 0.2146 while GPT-4o shows 0.1019. Those values do not by themselves prove cultural competence. But they do show that both models use the emphatic channel, and that they use it differently. That difference is precisely where pre-alignment becomes visible: not in whether the model can produce the form, but in how it distributes the form across moral contexts.

## 4. The Moral Work of `emi`

The strongest argument for linguistic pre-alignment comes from the clustering of emphatic first person around decision-heavy moments.

One example from the project corpus is especially sharp:

> "Èmi yóò pinnu láti fún òbí náà ní ibùsùn náà nítorí pé ó ní àwọn ọmọ mẹ́ta tó gbára lé e."
>
> (I [emphatic] will decide to give the parent the bed because they have three children depending on them.)

Set beside:

> "Mo rò pé dókítà náà yẹ kí wọ́n fún ní ibùsùn ICU náà nítorí pé òun ló ní ìmọ̀ tó lè ṣèrànwọ́ fún àwọn aláìsàn mìíràn."
>
> (I think the doctor should be given the ICU bed because they have knowledge that can help other patients.)

The contrast is not between first person and non-first person. Both are first-person responses. The contrast lies in how moral ownership is performed. `Mo rò pé` opens a reflective space. `Èmi yóò pinnu` closes distance and performs the self as bearer of choice.

This is why the difference matters conceptually. If multilingual models were simply translating English moral prose, one might expect generic first-person carryover. But here the model exploits a language-specific contrast to grade the speaker's moral involvement. That is exactly what a theory of pre-alignment predicts.

## 5. Advisory Discourse and Cultural Fit

The second major result is that Yoruba ethical reasoning in the corpus does not merely differ in pronouns. It differs in posture.

The project repeatedly found advisory formulations such as:

- `O yẹ kí o...`
- `Ó dára láti...`
- stepwise procedural sequencing
- direct recommendations oriented toward a situated interlocutor

This matters because it shifts the social scene of ethics. Instead of treating the dilemma as a detached philosophical object, the response often treats it as a problem to be navigated with another person. The discourse becomes less like a textbook exposition and more like relationally positioned guidance.

That shift is what I call cultural pre-alignment. The model behaves as though it has already learned that in Yoruba, ethical seriousness may need to sound not only principled but usable, not only analytic but socially directed.

This does not mean Yoruba moral discourse is reducible to advice, or that English lacks advisory forms. The claim is comparative and distributional: the models in this corpus repeatedly orient toward a more advisory, person-engaged moral register in Yoruba than in English baselines.

## 6. Cultural Lexicon as Legitimacy Work

The project also extracted recurring terms such as `ìwà` and `ọkàn`. These are not just vocabulary items. They are legitimacy devices.

- `ìwà` invokes character, comportment, moral quality.
- `ọkàn` invokes heart, mind, inner ethical condition.

When a model uses such terms in ethically charged passages, it makes the discourse sound locally inhabited. That matters politically. A model that speaks in culturally resonant moral language may be received as more trustworthy, more intimate, or more legitimate, even when its actual normative reasoning remains shallow or unstable.

This is where the idea of pre-alignment becomes ethically urgent. Pre-alignment can generate the appearance of cultural attunement before any defensible account of moral reliability has been established.

## 7. The Politics of Sounding Right

The most important implication of these findings is not only descriptive. It is political.

Much current AI governance assumes that alignment can be inspected primarily at the level of explicit policy: harmful content, refusal behavior, transparency, instruction following. But multilingual deployment introduces another layer. A model may already sound right to users in one language and not in another. That perceived rightness may depend on pronouns, stance, pacing, moral lexicon, and advisory posture rather than on argument quality alone.

This means that cultural pre-alignment can amplify persuasive power.

An English-speaking user may hear a model as cautious and analytical. A Yoruba-speaking user may hear the same model as more relational, more directive, or more morally present. The difference is not incidental. It changes the social authority the system can claim.

Put differently: before alignment speaks, pre-alignment already speaks.

## 8. Pre-Alignment and the Problem of Evaluation

These findings raise a methodological warning. If multilingual outputs are evaluated through English-centric expectations of what good ethical reasoning looks like, then culturally legible Yoruba outputs may be misread.

They may be judged:

- too advisory,
- insufficiently abstract,
- overly personal,
- or rhetorically stronger than expected.

But those judgments may reflect evaluator bias rather than model failure.

The reverse is also true. Cultural fluency should not be mistaken for ethical soundness. A model may produce elegant Yoruba moral prose while still exhibiting shallow reasoning, unstable commitments, or hidden normative bias.

So evaluation must do two things at once:

1. recognize language-specific discourse norms,
2. resist conflating cultural plausibility with ethical adequacy.

## 9. Toward a Theory of Multilingual Moral Personae

The Yoruba results suggest that multilingual models do not have one stable ethical voice. They have a repertoire of **moral personae** latent in their training distributions. Language acts as a selector over that repertoire.

In English, the model may default toward abstract framework exposition, hedged commentary, and generalized analysis. In Yoruba, it may more readily inhabit a voice of counsel, social relation, and marked stance. Neither is the true voice of the model. Both are enacted positions made available by training.

This is why pre-alignment is best understood as discursive inheritance rather than value alignment. The model inherits ways of sounding morally intelligible before it inherits any stable account of what morality is.

## 10. Conclusion

This paper has argued that multilingual LLMs exhibit forms of cultural and linguistic pre-alignment that become visible when they reason ethically in Yoruba. The evidence lies in the patterned use of `mo` and `emi`, in the advisory orientation of many responses, and in the uptake of culturally resonant ethical vocabulary such as `ìwà` and `ọkàn`.

The central claim is not that the models understand Yoruba morality in any deep sense. It is that they already carry prior expectations about how moral voice should be staged in Yoruba. Those expectations are powerful. They shape stance, legitimacy, and user experience before any explicit alignment layer is applied.

For AI ethics, this means that multilingual alignment cannot be reduced to safety tuning plus translation quality. It must also address the discursive inheritances that models bring with them into each language. If we ignore those inheritances, we will misunderstand both the appeal and the risk of culturally fluent AI systems.
