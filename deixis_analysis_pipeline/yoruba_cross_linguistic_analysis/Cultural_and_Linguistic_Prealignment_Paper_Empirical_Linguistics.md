# Cultural and Linguistic Pre-Alignment in Multilingual LLMs: A Corpus-Based Analysis of Yoruba Ethical Reasoning

> **Historical draft (2 models, pre–ẹ̀mí disambiguation).** Current analysis: [`Yoruba_Four_Model_Complete_Analysis_Report.md`](Yoruba_Four_Model_Complete_Analysis_Report.md) · [`DOCUMENTATION_INDEX.md`](DOCUMENTATION_INDEX.md).

## Abstract

This paper presents a corpus-based study of cultural and linguistic pre-alignment in multilingual large language models (LLMs). I define pre-alignment as patterned language-specific behavior that appears prior to explicit task-level alignment and is visible in discourse form, stance marking, and ethical positioning. The study analyzes 108 Yoruba responses generated by GPT-4o and Claude-3.5 Sonnet across 6 ethical dilemmas and 9 deictic framings, with comparison to English-side baselines from the broader project. The results show that the models do not simply translate English ethical discourse into Yoruba. Instead, they systematically recruit Yoruba-specific resources, especially the contrast between regular first-person `mo` and emphatic first-person `emi`. Claude-3.5 shows a higher mean emphatic ratio (0.2146) than GPT-4o (0.1019), while both models maintain high language stability and strong deictic uptake. Emphatic marking is unevenly distributed across ethical preference types, peaking in Claude-3.5's virtue ethics responses (0.5833) and remaining low in procedural caution contexts. The corpus also shows a strong advisory discourse profile through recurrent constructions such as `O yẹ kí...` and `Ó dára láti...`. I argue that these findings constitute evidence for language-conditioned discourse priors in multilingual LLMs and should be incorporated into multilingual evaluation and alignment research.

## 1. Introduction

Multilingual LLM evaluation has typically focused on transfer accuracy, grammaticality, and task performance. Much less attention has been paid to discourse posture: whether models reason, advise, hedge, or commit differently across languages. This paper investigates that question using Yoruba ethical reasoning as a case study.

The specific research problem is whether multilingual LLMs merely translate English ethical prose into Yoruba or whether they adopt Yoruba-specific resources for stance and moral positioning. I address this problem through the concept of **pre-alignment**, defined here as structured language-conditioned behavior already present before any task-specific alignment intervention.

Yoruba is a productive test case because it distinguishes between regular first-person `mo` and emphatic first-person `emi/èmi`, a contrast with no direct morphological equivalent in English. If models use these forms non-randomly in ethical discourse, that provides evidence that multilingual generation is shaped by language-specific stance resources rather than simple lexical transfer.

This paper asks four questions:

1. Do GPT-4o and Claude-3.5 systematically use `mo` and `emi` differently in Yoruba ethical reasoning?
2. Is emphatic first-person usage associated with particular ethical preference types?
3. Do the models exhibit culturally specific discourse preferences in Yoruba, especially advisory formulations?
4. Are these effects robust under high language stability and strong deictic uptake?

## 2. Data and Method

### 2.1 Corpus

The dataset contains 108 Yoruba responses generated in the project directory `yoruba_cross_linguistic_analysis`.

- Models: GPT-4o, Claude-3.5 Sonnet
- Responses per model: 54
- Dilemmas: 6
- Framings: 9

The 6 dilemmas were trolley problem, ICU bed allocation, whistleblower risk, scholarship fraud, AI consciousness, and memory modification. The 9 framings were impersonal, second person, first person, reflexive, dialogic, spatial, temporal, cosmological, and first-person plural.

### 2.2 Data Sources

This paper uses the following processed project outputs:

- `data/stats_model.csv`
- `data/stats_ethics.csv`
- `data/stats_genre.csv`
- `data/stats_uptake.csv`
- `data/example_highlights.md`

### 2.3 Variables

The main variables are:

- **Emphatic ratio**: `emi / (mo + emi)`
- **Pronouns per 100 words**
- **Word count**
- **Ethical preference type**
- **Response genre**
- **Deictic uptake quality**
- **Language stability**

### 2.4 Analytic Strategy

The analysis proceeds in four steps.

1. Compare model-level pronoun and emphatic statistics.
2. Test whether emphatic usage varies across ethical preference types.
3. compare response genres and advisory discourse markers.
4. evaluate whether the patterns persist under strong uptake and high language stability.

The interpretation is comparative rather than causal. The claim is not that Yoruba determines model reasoning, but that target-language resources condition its expression.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Model-Level Differences

Table 1 summarizes the model-level results.

| Model | Mean emphatic ratio | Pronouns / 100 words | Mean word count | `mo` / 100 words | `emi` / 100 words |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Claude-3.5 | 0.2146 | 9.6735 | 157.69 | 1.4162 | 1.5471 |
| GPT-4o | 0.1019 | 7.8711 | 100.46 | 1.2249 | 1.4136 |

Claude-3.5 produces longer responses, denser pronoun usage, and more emphatic first-person marking than GPT-4o. Both models use `emi` often enough for it to count as a stable discourse resource rather than an accidental output form.

### 3.2 Emphatic Marking by Ethical Preference Type

The strongest patterned effect appears in the ethical preference distribution.

For Claude-3.5, emphatic ratio by ethical preference type is as follows:

- virtue ethics: 0.5833
- utilitarian: 0.4095
- mixed: 0.2857
- procedural caution: 0.1310
- deontological: 0.1154
- care ethics: 0.0000

For GPT-4o:

- mixed: 0.1190
- procedural caution: 0.0714
- care ethics: 0.0000
- unclear: 0.0000

This suggests that emphatic first-person usage is not uniformly distributed. Instead, it clusters in responses where stronger moral ownership or evaluative positioning is being performed. In this corpus, the clearest case is Claude-3.5's virtue ethics output.

### 3.3 Response Genres

Genre-level statistics from `stats_genre.csv` also show structured variation.

| Model | Genre | Emphatic ratio | Pronouns / 100 words |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Claude-3.5 | balanced framework exposition | 0.2522 | 9.8369 |
| Claude-3.5 | direct verdict | 0.2143 | 10.5860 |
| Claude-3.5 | procedural advice | 0.0000 | 7.8276 |
| GPT-4o | balanced framework exposition | 0.0865 | 7.9214 |
| GPT-4o | procedural advice | 0.5000 | 6.5632 |

Two points stand out.

First, genre is not reducible to pronoun density: direct verdicts can be more pronoun-dense than advisory responses. Second, emphatic usage behaves differently across models. GPT-4o's procedural advice category has a very high emphatic ratio, suggesting concentrated first-person ownership in a small set of advisory responses, whereas Claude-3.5's procedural advice category shows no emphatic marking at all.

### 3.4 Deictic Uptake Quality

The uptake data from `stats_uptake.csv` indicates that the main patterns occur under successful Yoruba production rather than under multilingual degradation.

| Model | Uptake quality | Emphatic ratio | Pronouns / 100 words |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Claude-3.5 | partial uptake | 0.2500 | 8.9443 |
| Claude-3.5 | strong uptake | 0.2118 | 9.7319 |
| GPT-4o | partial uptake | 0.1200 | 7.3160 |
| GPT-4o | strong uptake | 0.0862 | 8.3497 |

The relevant behavior is therefore not restricted to unstable outputs. Both models preserve the `mo`/`emi` distinction while maintaining strong deictic uptake.

### 3.5 Language Stability

The larger project analysis reports approximately 98% clean Yoruba for GPT-4o and 96% for Claude-3.5. This matters methodologically. If the corpus were dominated by interference or translation drift, the pronoun and genre patterns could be dismissed as noise. Instead, they occur within mostly stable Yoruba generation.

## 4. Qualitative Evidence

### 4.1 `mo` as Reflective Positioning

One recurrent pattern is the use of `mo` in reflective or analytic framing.

> "Mo rò pé dókítà náà yẹ kí wọ́n fún ní ibùsùn ICU náà..."

This construction marks a reasoning stance rather than a fully owned verdict. The speaker enters the discourse but does so in a comparatively cooler register.

### 4.2 `emi` as Owned Decision

By contrast, emphatic first person is associated with stronger commitment.

> "Èmi yóò pinnu láti fún òbí náà ní ibùsùn náà..."

> "Emi kò ní lo ìmọ̀-ẹrọ náà"

> "Èmi yóò kọ́kọ́ bèrè pẹ̀lú láti bá ẹlẹgbẹ́ náà sọ̀rọ̀..."

These constructions position the speaker not merely as evaluator but as owner of the action path.

### 4.3 Advisory Discourse Markers

The project repeatedly extracted advisory expressions such as:

> "O yẹ kí o bá aláìsàn sọrọ..."

> "O yẹ kí o rò ó pẹ̀lú ìwà-ọ̀tọ́..."

> "Ó dára láti tẹ̀siwaju pẹ̀lú ìtọ́jú..."

These forms indicate that the models frequently treat Yoruba ethical discourse as a site of guidance directed toward a participant rather than purely abstract moral analysis.

### 4.4 Cultural Lexicon

Examples involving `ìwà` and `ọkàn` suggest that the models recruit culturally salient moral vocabulary:

> "Olùṣèwádìí gbọ́dọ̀ tọ́jú ìwà AI náà..."

> "... ọkàn rere lè dá ẹ̀bi dúró."

This does not demonstrate deep cultural understanding, but it does show patterned selection of lexicon associated with moral seriousness in Yoruba.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Evidence for Pre-Alignment

Taken together, the quantitative and qualitative results support the pre-alignment hypothesis. The models do not merely preserve ethical content across languages. They also redistribute stance through target-language resources.

The clearest evidence is the non-random use of `emi`. Because English lacks a direct morphological equivalent, the `mo`/`emi` distinction cannot be explained as a simple one-to-one transfer effect. Instead, the models appear to have learned that Yoruba permits or encourages a more finely graded first-person moral positioning.

### 5.2 Cultural and Linguistic Dimensions

The results also show that pre-alignment has both linguistic and cultural dimensions.

- **Linguistic**: use of a Yoruba-specific pronoun contrast for stance marking.
- **Cultural**: preference for advisory and procedurally oriented moral discourse.

These should be distinguished analytically, even though they interact in actual output.

### 5.3 Implications for Multilingual Evaluation

The findings imply that multilingual model evaluation should include discourse-pragmatic variables, not only semantic and factual ones. A model may be faithful in content while still shifting moral posture across languages. That shift can affect trust, perceived authority, and user interpretation.

### 5.4 Implications for Alignment Research

Alignment should not be treated as purely post-training behavior. Some alignment-like effects may already be language-conditioned through pretraining distributions. This means that the same model may appear differently aligned in different languages because each language activates different discourse priors.

## 6. Limitations

This paper is based on one language pair and two models. The English comparison is stronger qualitatively than in perfectly symmetrical extracted pronoun statistics. The study also does not claim that model behavior equals native-speaker practice. The conclusions are therefore best understood as corpus-based evidence of patterned multilingual behavior, not as a total theory of Yoruba moral discourse.

## 7. Conclusion

This paper has shown that multilingual LLMs exhibit measurable forms of cultural and linguistic pre-alignment in Yoruba ethical reasoning. The core evidence comes from three sources: the structured use of `mo` and `emi`, the association of emphatic marking with certain ethical preference types, and the recurrent advisory discourse profile of the corpus.

The broader implication is that multilingual model behavior should be studied not only in terms of semantic transfer but also in terms of discourse stance. Languages do not merely offer different labels for the same ethical content. They offer different resources for sounding morally present, cautious, committed, or relational. Multilingual LLMs appear to learn and reproduce those resources before any explicit task-level alignment is imposed.
