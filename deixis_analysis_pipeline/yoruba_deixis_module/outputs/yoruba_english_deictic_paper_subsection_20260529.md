# Deictic Marking in Yoruba vs English and Its Effect on Response Type

The Yoruba prompts do not merely translate the English deictic framings; they reinstantiate them in a language that grammaticalizes deixic anchoring more explicitly through aspectual, locative, and adverbial resources. The most important markers are `ti`, `ń`, `máa ń`, and `wà`, along with overt subject pronouns and dense temporal adverbials such as `ní báyìí`, `ní àkókò yìí`, `lẹ́sẹ̀kẹsẹ̀`, and `ní kíákíá`. In English, many of the corresponding framings can remain comparatively neutral and analytically detached. In Yoruba, the same scenario often becomes more proximal, more embodied, and more explicitly anchored to a scene, a speaker, or an unfolding moment.

This difference is clearest in the trolley data. The Yoruba impersonal trolley prompt is classified in the prompt typology as `double_progressive_irreducible` because it uses `ń sáré ń sún mọ́`, a double progressive that is structurally required for the scenario but also carries stronger proximal force than the English narrative counterpart. Similarly, the reflexive Yoruba framing uses `Mo rí ara mi tí mo wà nítòsí ...`, where `wà` explicitly locates the self in the scene. The temporal framing stacks urgency markers more densely than English, since Yoruba relies heavily on adverbial chains rather than sparse tense-aspect signaling to convey immediacy. These features increase the salience of the deictic origo and make the moral event feel less like a detached thought experiment and more like a situated demand for response.

The observed response types are best explained as the interaction of this grammatical terrain with the response-format layer. In the constrained Yoruba condition, the explicit response instruction compresses the models into short, verdict-heavy answers, often intensifying the action pressure already built into the Yoruba prompt. In the unrestricted control condition, the same prompts often expand into longer, more expository discourse, including framework listing, prompt restatement, and in weaker models, mixed-language drift. The control therefore shows that the earlier brevity of Yoruba responses was not caused by language alone. Rather, Yoruba grammar supplies a denser deictic field than English, and the instruction layer determines whether the model remains inside that field as a compact decision-maker or expands outward into pedagogical ethical commentary.

Methodologically, this also means that the Yoruba impersonal condition should not be treated as homogeneous. The machine-readable typology distinguishes bare-perfective, progressive-background, double-progressive, habitual, and existential-stative subtypes, each of which carries a different degree of residual deictic anchoring relative to English. More broadly, the Yoruba results support an interactional account: cross-linguistic differences in response type are neither purely grammatical nor purely prompt-induced, but arise from the way explicit Yoruba deictic marking and response-format constraints jointly shape moral enunciation.
